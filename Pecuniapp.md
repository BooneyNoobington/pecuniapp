# Pecuniapp

## What it is

With "Pecunia" being Latin for "money", Pecuniapp is a personal financial tool that aims for quick access, simplicity and ease of use. Unlike more professional, feature-packed apps like "Firefly III", Pecuniapp is designed to be brought up on your phone's screen while you're in line at the cashier and have everything entered before you need to pick up your shopping bag again.

This means that things like double-entry bookkeeping take a back seat in favour of everything a regular user will care about.

## Target Audience

You. Not as an employee of an enterprise but as a private person. What once were spreadsheets is now packaged into a neat app – represented by understandable relational tables.

## OS compatibility

Pecuniapp is a free and open source app for the Apple ecosystem, requiring iOS 26 or macOS 26 or later. Expansion into web app, Android and Windows territory is on the horizon but nothing concrete yet.

The code is developed in `Swift` but with portability in mind.

## Backend(s)

Pecuniapp always stores your data in a local SQLite database on your device, so entering something never has to wait for a network. From there, you choose:

- **Local:** your data stays on that device. Sync is simply switched off.
- **Self-hosted cloud:** your devices also sync through your own `PocketBase` server (https://pocketbase.io). If the server can't be reached, you won't notice: changes wait on the device and go out the next time it can.

Switching from local to cloud later is just a first upload. By using established open source software as a backend, your data stays yours, not only in name but in fact.

The app only reaches its backend through one small interface (see [Sync](#sync)). If PocketBase ever becomes unavailable, that junction is the only part that needs to be rewritten.

## Database Buildup

Every synced record has an ID generated by the device and a `deleted_at` date instead of ever being physically deleted. The database doesn't enforce references between synced records, because a record can arrive before the one it refers to (see [What happens when...](#what-happens-when)).

On the device, the data lives in SQLite. Each synced field is a row of its own, together with the timestamp that decides merges, so a new field never needs a database migration. The readable tables, such as accounts and transactions, are views on top of these rows.

### Accounts

Your bank, your eBay balance or even just the loose change in your pocket.

Each account has a name, an opening balance and a currency. Totals are shown per currency and there is no conversion for now. An account's currency can't be changed once it's in use.

Balances are never stored. They are always worked out from the opening balance, transactions, transfers and subscriptions, so they can't drift out of step.

Amounts are stored as whole numbers in the currency's smallest unit (cents, for example), never as floating-point numbers.

### Categories and Recipients

Categories are what tell the charts where your money went. Both categories and recipients are optional on every transaction, so they never slow you down at the cashier.

### Transactions

These represent the flow of your funds. Each transaction is related to an account. They can be positive or negative. Think of your salary as a subscription your company has towards you. The guy that bought your chess set from you on Craigslist because, like me, you never managed to cross the 700 ELO barrier, technically also contributes to your income.

#### One-Time

You walk home, walk by the bakery and decide to grab a croissant which you pay for with your loose change. You don't know when you'll buy your next one and you don't even care that much which bakery you bought from. Therefore the recipient is not mandatory here.

#### Subscriptions

Since everything is one nowadays, you probably have those too. Even if you just get billed for electricity once a month. Subscriptions are defined in their own table, have a mandatory start date and an optional end. Pecuniapp works out on its own how to display them in the ledger and how they affect your balance.

**Versions:** prices change, so a subscription has one or more versions, each with a `valid_from` date, an amount and a schedule. To enter a price change, you give the new price and the date. A version applies until the next one starts, so the old one ends automatically and no gaps or overlaps can occur. A price change keeps the existing billing rhythm, while a schedule change starts a new one from its `valid_from` date.

**Overrides:** the one electricity bill that came in higher or the month you skipped is stored as an override for that specific occurrence.

**Occurrences** are calculated for display and never stored as rows, so two devices can't each create their own copy. An occurrence is identified by its subscription and calendar date. Dates are always calculated from the schedule's first date, so a subscription starting on January 31st is billed on the last day of February and still on March 31st.

### Transfers

Getting cash from the ATM moves money from your bank account to your loose change. You didn't spend anything, so transfers have their own table and stay out of the income and spending charts. A transfer stores an amount for each side, so it also works between currencies.

### The Ledger

The main part of the app that shows exactly what happened. It can be filtered by various parameters such as recipient or account. One-time transactions can be entered here directly.

## Sync

The goal: any number of your devices can work offline, in any order, and all of them end up with exactly the same data without losing anything.

Syncing whole files, as `rsync` does, can't achieve this, since a file only tells you that it changed and not what changed. So Pecuniapp doesn't sync its tables at all. It syncs a log of changes.

### How it works

1. Each write to the local database also adds an entry to a local change log, in the same database transaction. An entry holds the record, the changed fields, their new values and a timestamp.
2. Each device numbers its own entries 1, 2, 3 and so on, without gaps. Each entry also has a random ID and names the entry before it, so identical entries always have identical histories.
3. Pushing means encrypting and sending the entries the server doesn't have yet. The server only ever appends them.
4. Pulling means asking for every other device's entries beyond the last number seen from that device. Everything received is applied in one database transaction.
5. For each field, the value with the newest timestamp wins, and the same rule runs on every device. Since the outcome doesn't depend on the order entries arrive in, all devices end up the same.

Each write is stamped with the current time or, if that's later, just past the timestamp of the values it overwrites. An edit therefore always beats the value you were looking at, even if the device's clock is wrong. Timestamps are only ever compared per field, so a device with a wrong date can only affect the fields it edits. Ties are broken by device ID and then entry number, so the order is always strict.

The numbering, not the clock, decides what still needs to be transferred, so a wrong clock can never cause a change to be skipped.

### What happens when...

| Situation                                                    | Result                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| You enter different things on your phone and your iMac       | Both are kept. New records have their own IDs, so they can't collide. |
| You change the amount on one device and the category on another | Both changes are kept, because merging happens per field.    |
| You change the same field on two devices                     | The newest change wins on all devices. The older value stays in the log and can be shown and restored. |
| You delete a record on one device and edit it on another     | It stays deleted, because the edit doesn't touch `deleted_at`. You can restore it, including the edit. |
| Two devices enter a price change or override for the same subscription and date | Their IDs are derived from the subscription and date, so they merge into one record. |
| You enter the same expense on both devices                   | See below.                                                   |
| You merge the same duplicate on two devices                  | The merge always keeps the record with the lowest ID, so both devices make the same choice and one record remains. |
| The connection dies during a push                            | Before each push, the device asks for the highest number the server has from it, and sends what's missing. Retrying is harmless. |
| A device is restored from an old backup                      | The server has higher numbers from this device than the device itself does. It takes a new device ID, so it can't reuse old numbers, and then pulls everything. |
| You restore a backup onto a new device and keep using the old one | Both would have the same device ID and could give different entries the same number, which comparing numbers doesn't always reveal. So each device also keeps its ID in its Keychain, which doesn't move with a backup. If that ID doesn't match the one in the database, the database is a copy, and the device takes a new ID before writing anything. |
| You restore a device from a backup and enter something before it syncs | The device reuses numbers it had already used, so one number now means two different things. Before syncing, a device compares its entry at the highest number both sides have with the server's, for every device it knows. If they differ, the history has split. The server's side stays as it is, and whatever only exists on this side is re-issued under the device's own ID with its original timestamps, so nothing is lost. |
| The server is restored from an old backup                    | The server is missing entries that devices already have. Before each push, a device compares the highest number on the server for every device it knows, not only itself, and uploads what's missing. Entries stay valid because they're authenticated by device ID and number, not by who uploads them. So any one remaining device can rebuild the whole log, even on an empty server, including the entries of retired devices. |
| A device's clock is far in the future                        | Only the fields it edits get a future timestamp. Later edits go just past that timestamp and win, and everything else stays on real time, so correcting the date fixes the rest. An edit made elsewhere without having seen the future value loses to it, but the older value stays in the log and can be restored. The sync status warns when a device's clock differs a lot from the server's. |
| A change comes from a newer app version                      | It's stored but not applied until the app is updated, and the app asks you to update. |
| A record refers to one that hasn't arrived yet               | This is normal, because each device's entries arrive separately. Nothing fails, since the database doesn't enforce these references. The app shows the missing record as unknown until it arrives. |

### Duplicates

Two records for the same purchase can't be detected by sync, because you may really have bought two coffees that morning. Pecuniapp therefore never removes them on its own. Records with the same account and amount, dated within a day of each other and with no conflicting recipient, are shown as possible duplicates. You merge or dismiss them with one tap, and that decision syncs like everything else.

A merge always keeps the record with the lowest ID and marks the other as deleted, after copying over any field that's empty on the kept record. If each device kept the record it was showing, two devices could delete each other's choice and the purchase would disappear. If one device merges while another dismisses, the merge stands, and the deleted record can still be restored.

### Backend interface

The rest of the app knows nothing about PocketBase. It only uses this interface:

- Log in
- Store and fetch the wrapped encryption keys
- Register a device
- List the user's devices
- Get the newest entry stored for a device
- Push entries
- Pull a device's entries after a given number

### On the server

PocketBase holds user logins and exactly three collections, which never change as the app's data model evolves. This means that app updates need no server migrations.

| Collection  | Fields                                                       | Rules                                                        |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `devices`   | `user`, `device_id`, `encrypted_name`                        | Owner can read and create                                    |
| `changes`   | `user`, `device_id`, `seq`, `payload`                        | Owner can read and create. Nobody except a superuser can update or delete. |
| `user_keys` | `user`, `kdf_salt`, `kdf_params`, `key_generation`, `key_from_passphrase`, `key_from_recovery` | Owner can read, create and update                            |

`changes` has a unique index on `user`, `device_id` and `seq`, and `devices` has one on `user` and `device_id`.

Your readable tables therefore live on your devices, while the server stores the log. PocketBase's dashboard won't show your transactions as a table, so the app provides CSV and SQLite exports.

The log isn't compacted for now. A household's entries are small enough for this not to matter, even on a Raspberry Pi. A new device simply replays the log from the start.

### Multiple users

One server can serve several people, each with their own login and completely separate data. Nothing is shared between them, so their data can't conflict. Public sign-up stays disabled and the server's admin creates the accounts.

A PocketBase superuser ignores all access rules, which is one reason everything is encrypted on the device: whoever runs the server only sees ciphertext (see [End-to-end encryption](#end-to-end-encryption)).

### Testing

Sync gets more testing than any other part of the app. A simulation creates several virtual devices that make random changes, go offline, crash mid-push, have wrong clocks, get restored from backups (onto themselves or onto new devices) and receive entries in random order. The server gets restored from old backups too. After every run, all devices must hold identical data, rebuilding the tables from the log must give the same result, nothing a remaining device ever held may be lost, and a brand-new device must be able to get everything from the server.

Every row in the table above also has its own test. To check that all these tests actually notice problems, `Tools/mutation_test.py` breaks the sync code on purpose, one rule at a time, and runs them against each broken version.

### To verify in the first prototype

These are assumptions the design relies on, which PocketBase's documentation doesn't clearly settle:

- `/api/batch` enforces each collection's API rules, so a user can't write entries in another user's name.
- A unique index across `user`, `device_id` and `seq` works as expected.
- The `payload` text field can be configured to hold the largest entries.
- The batch limits can be configured to work on a Raspberry Pi. Batch requests are disabled by default and need to be enabled during setup.
- A maintained Swift wrapper for `libsodium` works on all Apple target platforms.
- The Argon2id settings stay usable on the oldest supported phone while still being expensive to attack.
- PocketBase can serve HTTPS with your own certificate on a bare IP address. Its production guide only describes automatic Let's Encrypt certificates for a domain. If it can't, the setup tools also have to install a reverse proxy such as `Caddy`.

### End-to-end encryption

Cloud mode is always encrypted, with no option to turn it off, so there's only one code path. Your data is encrypted on your device before it leaves, so the server admin and anyone holding the Pi's SD card only ever see ciphertext. Local mode sends nothing anywhere, so encryption begins when you first switch on sync.

**Two secrets:** your login password goes to the server, while your encryption passphrase never leaves your device. They are deliberately separate, so the server can never receive what it would need to decrypt your data.

**Keys:**

1. On first setup, the device generates a random data key, which encrypts all your data.
2. Argon2id turns your passphrase into a second key, which encrypts the data key. This wrapped copy is stored in `user_keys`, and it's what lets a new device join using only your passphrase.
3. The device also generates a recovery key, shows it once for you to print or store safely, and stores a second wrapped copy.
4. Each device keeps the data key in its Keychain, available after first unlock so sync can run in the background, and limited to that device so it isn't included in backups.

**Encrypting entries:** each `payload` and device name is encrypted with XChaCha20-Poly1305 using a random nonce. For entries, the device ID and number are authenticated with the ciphertext, so the server can't swap, move or alter entries without the device noticing. Both are generated by the app rather than the server, so the log still decrypts after moving to another backend. Each `payload` also starts with an unencrypted header holding the format version and the key generation, both authenticated too. The key generation says which data key an entry uses, so a device can tell an old entry it should skip after a leak (see below) from one that's been tampered with. Each user has their own data key, so one user's entries can't be passed off as another's. The same `libsodium` library provides this and Argon2id on every platform Pecuniapp might reach later.

**Forgetting or changing your passphrase:**

| Situation                                                    | Result                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| You change your passphrase                                   | The data key is wrapped again. Nothing else needs re-encrypting. |
| Your passphrase has leaked                                   | Changing it isn't enough, because old server backups still contain the old wrapped copy. A trusted device creates a new data key and recovery key with the next key generation number, and starts a fresh log of your current data under a new device ID. Your other devices ask for the new passphrase, take new device IDs and re-send what the fresh log is missing. Old entries stay on the server, because only a superuser can delete them, and devices skip them. Older overwritten values aren't carried over. |
| You forget your passphrase, but a device is still set up     | That device sets a new one, because it still holds the data key. |
| You forget your passphrase, but you have the recovery key    | The recovery key unlocks the data key and you set a new passphrase. |
| You have lost the passphrase, the recovery key and all devices | Your data is gone. Nobody can recover it, by design.         |

**What it doesn't protect against:**

- **Metadata:** the server can still see who syncs, when, from how many devices, how many entries there are and how large they are.
- **Deletion:** a server admin can't read or undetectably alter your data, but can delete or withhold it. Your devices hold full copies, and exports stay available.
- **Weak passphrases:** anyone holding the server's data can try to guess your passphrase offline. Argon2id slows this down, so the app requires a strong passphrase.
- **A leaked passphrase:** changing it stops new use of that wrapped key, but old server backups still contain the old copy. A real leak requires a new data key, so version 1 starts a fresh log from a trusted device (see the table above). That protects what comes after it, but anyone holding the old entries and the leaked passphrase can still read them.
- **Your devices:** the local database and exports aren't covered by this. They rely on your device's own encryption and passcode.

## Niceties

Pecuniapp features the usual bells and whistles in regard to graphs and pie charts to tell you exactly where you overspent – in a nice SwiftUI interface to not make you feel too bad about it.

## Self-Hosting

To make using your own cloud storage as easy as possible, Pecuniapp comes with some tools. When for example all you have or want to invest is an old Raspberry Pi, reachable via IP and a self-signed certificate, you need some way to tell your phone to trust it without having to run a custom CA – which you absolutely can, of course. Pecuniapp respects your device's trust store but can also store the certificate of a custom CA such as `Step CA` or `mkcert` without having to have your entire device trust it.

The setup tools install the PocketBase version tested with your app release, create the collections, enable batch requests and disable public sign-up. To reach your server from outside your home, the documentation recommends a VPN such as `Tailscale` or `WireGuard` rather than exposing it to the internet.

Pecuniapp is designed to manage money, not make it. Which is why it's licensed under the MIT license. So, if it's any good, expect some commercial clones appearing. But be sure that the vanilla experience will always be here – ad free.
